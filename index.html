Slides 9–11 What ERP is — core concepts
▼

ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) is business management software that integrates all company functions into one system with a central database. Before ERP, every department had separate software that didn't talk to each other.

5 ERP characteristics — all apply directly to MM:

Integrated system

A GR (Goods Receipt) in MM auto-creates an accounting document in FI. Zero manual re-entry between modules.

Real time

Stock levels update the moment you post a goods movement. No batch delays. Inventory is always live.

Best practice

SAP enforces the P2P process: PR → PO → GR → Invoice → Payment. You can't skip steps without specific config.

Customizing (config)

MM is heavily configurable in IMG — plant parameters, valuation classes, movement types, number ranges, etc.

Process oriented

MM is built around Procure-to-Pay. Not around individual functions — around end-to-end business processes.

ERP components simplified (slide 11):

Enterprise

A firm that buys, makes, sells, ships products or services

Resource

Factors of production

Land, Labor, Capital. In MM context: materials and inventory.

Planning

Organizing activities to achieve a goal

In MM: MRP, procurement planning, stock planning.

Integration

One database, every department, no silos

Slides 12–13 ERP evolution — why MRP history matters for MM
▼
1970s

MRP — Material Requirements Planning

Factory material inventory. Calculate what materials to order and when. This is the direct ancestor of SAP MM's MRP functionality.

1980s

MRP II — Manufacturing Resource Planning

Expanded beyond materials to all factory resources — machines, labor, capacity. Added production scheduling.

1990s

ERP — Enterprise Resource Planning

Added departmental modules: HR, general ledger, purchase orders, CRM. This is when SAP R3 was the dominant player.

2000s

ERP II — Extended ERP

ERP + external actors. Added CRM, SCM, and other stakeholder modules. SAP Business Suite era.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR MM

SAP MM's MRP (transactions MD01/MD02) is the direct descendant of the 1970s MRP concept. When you run MRP in SAP, you're executing the same logic: calculate what materials are needed, when, and in what quantities. Understanding this history makes MRP click faster — it's 50+ years of manufacturing planning logic baked into software.

COMMON MISTAKE

Use the Shortcuts tab only. The System tab is for changing clients or password resets — don't click it to log on.

FIRST LOGIN — MANDATORY PASSWORD RESET

The first time you log in, SAP immediately displays a dialog requiring you to set a new password. Enter the new password in both the "New password" and "Repeat password" fields, then click Confirm. You cannot skip this step.

Password rules (slide 8)

Allowed

Letters a–z, numbers 0–9, punctuation

SAP R/3 does not distinguish upper and lower case — "Password" and "PASSWORD" are identical.

Not allowed as first character

?, !, or a blank space

Cannot start with 3 identical characters (e.g. "fff…"). Cannot contain 3 consecutive characters from your user name. Cannot reuse your last 5 passwords. Do not use "pass".

Logging off (slide 14)

Use System → Log Off or the Exit icon. Closing the window without logging off leaves your session open on the server — a security risk.

Day 4 — MM Procurement Basics

Slide 2 Organizational structure — definitions
▼

Every SAP transaction requires you to specify an organizational level. Knowing what each level is and what it controls is non-negotiable.

Client

Corporate group level

Highest level in ECC. Has its own data, master records, and table set. One SAP system = one client.

Company Code

Legal / accounting unit

Smallest unit with its own independent accounting. Financial statements are produced at Company Code level.

Plant

Operational logistics unit

Structures the enterprise from production, procurement, and materials planning perspectives. A manufacturing facility or branch within a company.

Storage Location (SLOC)

Physical stock area within plant

Differentiates material stocks within a plant. Inventory Management is carried out at SLOC level.

Purchasing Organization

Negotiates with vendors

Negotiates purchase conditions for one or more plants. Legally responsible for completing purchasing contracts. Key MM unit.

Purchasing Group

Buyer / buyer team

A buyer or group of buyers responsible for certain purchasing activities. Assigned on PO line items.

MEMORY TIP

Client → Company Code → Plant → Storage Location is the inventory hierarchy. Purchasing Org and Purchasing Group are the procurement responsibility hierarchy. Both hierarchies intersect at the Plant level.

▼

This is how the organizational levels nest inside each other in a real SAP system. One client → multiple company codes. One company code → multiple plants. One plant → multiple storage locations.

SLOC codes can repeat across plants — SLOC 0001 in Plant 1010 and SLOC 0001 in Plant 1020 are completely separate stock locations. The combination of Plant + SLOC uniquely identifies a stock location.

Slide 4 Purchasing organization — 3 setup types
▼

How you configure your Purchasing Organization determines which plants it can procure for. Three models:

1 — Central

One Purch. Org for all

A single purchasing org (e.g. P001) is responsible for the entire company. Used when purchasing is highly centralized.

2 — Plant-Specific

One Purch. Org per plant

Each plant has its own dedicated purchasing org. Used for decentralized, autonomous sites.

3 — Cross-Plant

One Purch. Org → multiple plants

One purchasing org assigned to multiple plants under the same company code. Most common in practice.

PRACTICAL NOTE

Cross-Plant is the most realistic setup for mid-to-large companies. A corporate purchasing team negotiates centrally and those contracts apply to all plants. Individual plants still do GRs and inventory management independently.

Slide 5 Core procurement cycle — the 4-step loop
▼

Slide 5 shows the basic procurement loop as a cycle. Slides 6 and 7 expand it with more steps.

Step 1

Purchase Requisitions (PREQ)

Internal request to procurement to buy something. Triggers the whole cycle.

Step 2

Purchase Orders (PO)

Formal legal commitment sent to vendor. Created from the PREQ.

Step 3

Goods Receipts (GR)

Physical delivery arrives. Stock increases. Accounting document auto-generated.

Step 4

Invoice Verification

Vendor's invoice checked against PO and GR. Approved → payment via FI.

It's a cycle because invoice verification feeds back — approved payments inform future vendor selection, and MRP forecasts drive the next round of requisitions.

MRP trigger (slide 6)

MRP runs in PP module and calculates what to procure based on demand, forecast, or independent requirements. Auto-generates PREQs — this is how most PREQs are created in real companies.

Inbound Delivery (slide 7)

Optional step between PO and GR. Vendor sends advance shipping notification (ASN). You create an Inbound Delivery to plan the receipt before goods physically arrive.

Invoice verification feeds back to vendor performance data, which informs future source determination and vendor selection. Procurement is never truly linear — it's an ongoing loop.

3-WAY MATCH — CRITICAL CONCEPT

PO → GR → Invoice. All three must match on quantity and price within tolerance. If the vendor invoices for more than what was received or at a different price than the PO, SAP blocks the invoice for review. This is a core internal control in MM.

Slide 9 detail Requisition determination — how needs are identified
▼

Requisition Determination is the upstream process of figuring out what needs to be procured. It feeds into the PREQ creation step.

Demand-driven

A sales order in SD triggers PP. PP runs MRP, determines material shortages, and auto-creates PREQs in MM. Full chain: SD → PP → MM.

Internal request

A department (IT, HR, maintenance) submits a PREQ manually. Goes through approval workflow before purchasing creates a PO.

Reorder point planning

If stock falls below the reorder point set in the Material Master, MRP auto-generates a PREQ. Common for raw materials with predictable consumption.

Forecast-based

Historical consumption patterns used to forecast future requirements. MRP uses these to plan procurement in advance of actual demand.

MM-PP INTEGRATION POINT

MRP is technically a PP function but directly outputs PREQs into MM. This is one of the most important cross-module integration points you'll encounter. When PP runs MRP, MM is the recipient of the resulting procurement signals.
